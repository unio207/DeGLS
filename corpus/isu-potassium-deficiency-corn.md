# Potassium deficiency in corn (not a disease)

Summarized from Iowa State University Extension and Outreach Integrated Crop Management.

## Symptoms and which leaves

Potassium deficiency appears first on the **lower, older** leaves, because potassium moves
easily within the plant from older to younger leaves. The symptom is yellowing progressing to
necrosis (tissue death) of the **outer leaf margins**, beginning at the leaf tip and running
down the edges toward the leaf base. In severe cases the leaf edges turn brown. Growers often
call this "firing" of the lower leaves.

## Distinguishing it from nitrogen deficiency

Both show on older, lower leaves, so position on the plant does not separate them. The
position **on the leaf** does:

- **Potassium**: yellowing/browning along the **outer margins**, edges inward.
- **Nitrogen**: yellowing from the leaf tip **down the midrib** in a V shape, center outward.

## Timing

Symptoms typically emerge after the V6 growth stage, roughly four to six weeks after planting,
because potassium uptake accelerates rapidly after about V6 as demand peaks.

## Moisture can cause false symptoms

Dry spring conditions can trigger temporary potassium deficiency symptoms even where soil
potassium is adequate, because moisture stress limits root uptake. Persistent symptoms
indicate a genuine soil deficiency, which is addressed with management such as fall potassium
banding in conservation tillage systems.

## Contrast with foliar disease

Marginal firing is a continuous band along the leaf edge, not a discrete lesion. Gray leaf
spot, northern corn leaf blight, and rust all produce bounded lesions or raised pustules on
tissue that is otherwise green, and they are not confined to the leaf margin.
