# What's lurking in the leaves this July? Corn diseases to scout for in Illinois

Following wet spring and early summer conditions, corn fields entering critical reproductive
stages face heightened disease risk. Plants may appear healthy from a distance while fungal and
soilborne pathogens are already active, often before visible symptoms emerge.

## Gray leaf spot

**Causal agent:** *Cercospora zeae-maydis*

**History and prevalence:** First reported in Illinois in 1924. Increasingly common in Midwest
corn fields, particularly in continuous corn production.

**Symptom identification:** Initial symptoms appear on lower leaves two to three weeks before
tasseling as small pinpoint lesions with brown halos. As the disease progresses, lesions become
rectangular, grey or brown, parallel to leaf veins, and measure approximately 1.5 to 2 inches
long.

**Spread:** Fungal spores overwinter in corn residue and disperse via wind or rain.

**Favorable conditions:** High temperatures (80 °F or higher) combined with high humidity (90
percent or higher) for approximately 12 hours.

**Yield impact:** Losses depend on leaf area affected. If less than 5% of leaf area is infected,
yield losses could be around 2%. If 25% to 75% of the leaf area is infected, yield loss could be
between 5% and 20%.

**Management:** Crop rotation, tillage, and foliar fungicides are effective control practices.

## Northern corn leaf blight

**Causal agent:** *Exserohilum turcicum*

**Symptom identification:** Infections begin on lower leaves and progress upward. Early signs are
narrow, long tan lesions parallel to leaf veins. As the disease develops, lesions enlarge and
resemble cigars, oblong and brown/tan.

**Spread:** Spores overwinter on corn residue and spread via wind or rain.

**Favorable conditions:** Extended wet periods (minimum 6 hours) combined with moderate
temperatures ranging from 61 to 84 °F.

**Timing and yield impact:** Disease timing dramatically affects yield loss. If lesions are
present on leaves next to developing ears approximately one to two weeks before and after
tasseling, yields could be reduced up to 30%.

**Management:** Resistant hybrids and foliar fungicide applications provide the most effective
control. Crop rotation offers limited effectiveness due to multiple fungal races.

## Tar spot

**Causal agent:** *Phyllachora maydis*

**Symptom identification:** Small, raised black lesions on leaves, frequently with brown halos.
Lesions with halos are termed "fisheyes."

**Favorable conditions:** Moderate temperatures (60-70 °F), high humidity (75 percent or
greater), and prolonged leaf wetness (minimum seven hours). Irrigated fields or areas near river
bottoms face elevated susceptibility.

**Yield impact:** Highly variable by field and infection severity. In some severe cases, yield
losses can exceed 50 to 60 bushels per acre. Reduced photosynthetic capacity also increases
lodging risk and decreases stalk integrity.

**Management:** Foliar fungicides applied between VT and R2 growth stages show greatest
effectiveness. Crop rotation and tillage provide minimal results. No resistant hybrids currently
exist.

## Scouting recommendations

Timely field observation is a critical management tool. Walk your fields, check the canopy, and
don't wait for symptoms to reach the ear leaf before making a management decision. The
University of Illinois Plant Clinic offers disease identification services for submitted samples.
