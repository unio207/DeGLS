# An Overview of Northern Corn Leaf Blight

DOI: doi.org/10.31274/cpn-20230425-0

Authors: Kiersten Wise (University of Kentucky), Gary Bergstrom (Cornell University), Trey
Price (Louisiana State University), Alison Robertson (Iowa State University), Albert Tenuta
(Ontario Ministry of Agriculture).

## Introduction

Northern corn leaf blight is a significant yield-limiting disease across the United States and
Canada. Despite its regional name, it affects a broad geographic area extending into the
southeastern U.S.

## Symptoms and signs

Symptom expression varies with hybrid resistance and with the genotype of *Exserohilum
turcicum* present. On susceptible hybrids, initial symptoms are small, light-colored elliptical
lesions that develop parallel to leaf margins or veins, with water-soaked edges. Lesions expand
progressively, sometimes reaching six inches long, and develop the characteristic "cigar shape"
associated with the disease.

As lesions mature they turn grayish or tan. Dark-gray or green fungal spore masses become
visible at the lesion center under magnification. Multiple lesions on a leaf may coalesce,
producing extensive leaf blighting.

On resistant hybrids, partial resistance produces small, restricted lesions with reduced spore
production. These may appear as small, narrow yellow or chlorotic streaks rather than classic
cigar-shaped lesions, which can make field identification difficult.

## Disease cycle

*E. turcicum* overwinters on corn residue. In fields with substantial residue, the first
lesions typically appear on lower leaves as spores are moved by rainsplash and wind from
residue. Secondary spore dispersal drives progression through the middle and upper canopy when
conditions are favorable. Wind-borne spores can travel between fields, so symptoms sometimes
appear first in the upper canopy when the inoculum came from outside the field. NCLB symptoms
can appear throughout the season but are typically observed from the late vegetative stages
(V10-V14) through physiological maturity (R6).

## Conditions that favor disease

The pathogen requires relatively long periods of leaf moisture (6 hours) for infection, which
may be supplied by rain, heavy dew, or overhead irrigation. Optimal development occurs under
high environmental moisture and moderate temperatures (64 to 81 °F; 18 to 27 °C), though
infection can occur outside this range.

Under favorable moisture and temperature, lesions can develop within a week after infection.
Hot, dry weather reduces spread of the NCLB fungus and slows disease development.

## Yield losses and impact

Significant yield reduction occurs when lesions establish in the canopy prior to and
immediately following tasseling and conditions support continued disease progression. Up to 50
percent yield losses have been observed in susceptible hybrids if the disease is established
prior to tasseling. Late-season NCLB developing after the dough stage produces minimal yield
impact.

## Diagnosis

Mature lesions contain fungal spores at hyphal tips, visible at about 20x magnification. The
spores are dark gray-green and concentrated in the lesion center, not enclosed in any
additional fungal structure. Visibility increases after dew or rain; dry conditions and aged
lesions reduce spore visibility. Laboratory diagnosis can confirm uncertain field
identifications.

## Diseases with similar symptoms

**Diplodia leaf streak.** Diplodia lesions expand across leaf tissue from initial small lesions
and may contain concentric rings. They typically appear in the mid-to-lower canopy. Round
fungal structures visible to the naked eye — resembling pepper specks within lesions —
distinguish Diplodia from *E. turcicum* sporulation.

**Goss's wilt.** Early Goss's wilt lesions are water-soaked, with diagnostic black freckling
appearing at lesion margins as the disease progresses. Bacterial exudate produces a
characteristic shine on affected leaves. It occurs across the Corn Belt and Manitoba but rarely
in southern states. NCLB lacks the shiny bacterial exudate, and NCLB lesions show no freckling
or water-soaking.

**Southern corn leaf blight.** SCLB produces tan or brown rectangular lesions with brown
margins, frequently surrounded by yellow halos; characteristics vary by causal race. SCLB
lesions typically stay in the lower canopy and are smaller than NCLB lesions, though a
resistant hybrid's response to NCLB can resemble SCLB. Laboratory analysis may be needed for
definitive differentiation.

## Management

Planting less susceptible hybrids is among the most effective approaches. Both partial
resistance and race-specific (*Ht* gene) resistance are commercially available.

Obtaining specific information about which *Ht* genes are in a given hybrid remains difficult,
as does determining which race-specific resistance genes are needed in a particular region.
Research characterizing the race structure of *E. turcicum* in the United States is most likely
outdated, so consulting state Extension specialists about local pathogen populations is
important. Seed dealer discussions help in selecting hybrids with reliable NCLB resistance
ratings and documented resistance sources.

Because the pathogen overwinters on corn residue, disease risk is elevated under continuous
corn and conservation tillage. Crop rotation and residue management where possible can help
reduce in-field sources of primary inoculum. These practices alone are insufficient, because
wind-dispersed spores travel between fields. Conservation tillage should not be abandoned;
rather, it should be complemented by other management strategies.

Both foliar and at-plant fungicides are available for NCLB. Preventative applications are most
effective. Foliar fungicide timings targeting tasseling/silking (VT/R1) have been most
successful at reducing NCLB compared to a non-treated control in many university studies.
Current fungicide efficacy ratings appear in the annually updated Crop Protection Network
efficacy table.
