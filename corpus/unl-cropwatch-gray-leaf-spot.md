# Gray Leaf Spot — Nebraska Extension CropWatch

## Pathogen

The causal agent is *Cercospora zeae-maydis* Tehon & E. Y. Daniels (teleomorph:
*Mycosphaerella*). The fungus produces hyphae and conidia and is distributed throughout the Corn
Belt from the mid-Atlantic states to Nebraska and Colorado. Two distinguishable populations
exist based on laboratory growth rates, geographic distribution, and cercosporin toxin
production, though they show no difference in virulence.

## Disease symptoms

All corn hybrids and inbreds are susceptible to gray leaf spot (GLS). Early lesions can be
confused with eyespot (*Kabatiella*) or anthracnose (*Colletotrichum*) infections. Mature
lesions are rectangular in shape and restricted by leaf veins; reverse lighting reveals a yellow
halo on most hybrids.

As the disease progresses, lesions coalesce, causing extensive leaf blighting. In fields with
primary inoculum sources, symptoms originate on the lower leaves and progress up the plant as
infection cycles occur. Severe infections can result in blighting of the entire plant and
potential stalk lodging. Husk and sheath lesions are oval to ellipsoidal and typically not
diagnostic.

The infection-to-sporulation cycle requires 14-21 days on a susceptible hybrid and 21-28 days on
a tolerant hybrid.

## Favorable environmental conditions

- Temperature: 70-90 °F (25-32 °C)
- Relative humidity: greater than 90 percent for more than 12 hours
- Leaf wetness: more than 12 hours from dew, rain, or irrigation

## Management

### Genetic resistance

Hybrid tolerance and resistance are the best management strategy. Tolerant hybrids yield well
under moderate to high disease pressure.

### Cultural practices

Tillage that incorporates residue into the soil can reduce GLS risk through decomposition.
However, when combined with other management tactics — crop rotation, resistant hybrids, and
fungicides — the benefits of reduced tillage (water retention, erosion control) outweigh the GLS
risk.

Rotation to crops other than corn for at least one year in low-risk fields (minimum two years in
high-risk fields) may minimize the severity of GLS.

### Chemical control

Under high disease pressure, timely fungicide applications can greatly minimize impact on yield,
though applications are costly and are typically justified only when disease pressure is
elevated.
